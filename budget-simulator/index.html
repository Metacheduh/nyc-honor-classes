Budget Simulator
your first paycheck, budget and savings

Pick a job, see what actually lands in your account, then decide where it goes. Five short steps: a paycheck with 2026 taxes, needs, wants and savings, an emergency fund, a credit card, and what saving early turns into by 65.

2026 tax figures · checked October 14, 2026 against the IRS, SSA and NY Tax Department

This free simulator lets a student build a first paycheck from a New York hourly wage, split it into needs, wants and savings, set an emergency fund goal, see what a credit card minimum payment really costs, and watch monthly saving grow from age 16 or 25. It runs in your browser, needs no sign-up, and doesn't send or save anything you enter. Teachers: the one-period lesson plan and NYSED topic map are at the bottom.

The simulator needs JavaScript turned on. The lesson plan below works without it.

A real pay stub can differ, because your employer withholds income tax using the forms you fill out when you're hired (federal Form W-4 and New York Form IT-2104). Step 2 · Budgeting and money management

Needs, wants and savings

Needs are what you must pay: a phone bill, a MetroCard or OMNY rides, school costs, money you give at home. Wants are the rest: food out, games, clothes beyond the basics. Savings is money you pay to your future self first. Slide to split your monthly take-home of .

Faded bar: the 50/30/20 rule of thumb (50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings). It is a common starting point, not a rule anyone has to follow. A student living at home may have small needs and can save far more than 20%.

Step 3 · Saving

Emergency fund goal

An emergency fund is savings you don't touch except for a surprise: a cracked phone screen, a lost MetroCard, a bill you didn't expect. Pick a goal that would cover a surprise in your life.

Step 4 · Credit and debt management

The credit card trap

A card's minimum payment keeps your account in good standing, but it is set low on purpose. Put in a balance and see what paying only the minimum costs.

24% is an example. Every card states its own APR in its terms.

Or $25, whichever is more. Cards set their own formula.

Illustrative math: interest is added once a month at the APR divided by 12, no new purchases, no fees. Real cards usually figure interest daily, and a late payment can bring a fee and a higher rate.

Step 5 · Saving and investing

Start at 16 or start at 25

Money that is invested can earn a return, and next year the return earns a return too. That is compound growth. Compare the same monthly amount started at 16 and at 25, both stopping at 65.

An assumption to play with, not a promise. Investments can lose money, and real returns go up and down every year.

    For teachers: a one-period lesson

    New York requires personal finance instruction for high school students by the end of grade 12, starting in the 2026-27 school year, and each district picks its own materials (8 NYCRR 100.2(c)(13); NYSED). This simulator is a free supplement your class can use on phones, laptops or one projected screen. Students don't make accounts, and nothing they type leaves their browser.

    Lesson outline (45 minutes)

    0-5Warm-up. Ask: "You work 15 hours at $17. How much do you think you take home?" Students write a guess.
    5-15Step 1, paycheck. Compare the guess with take-home. Switch the dependent box and the region, and talk about where each deduction goes. Ask why New York State tax can start before federal tax for a student who is claimed as a dependent.
    15-23Step 2, budget. In pairs, list three needs and three wants, then set the sliders. Compare with the 50/30/20 rule of thumb and discuss why a student's split can differ.
    23-28Step 3, emergency fund. Each student picks a goal and reads how many months it takes. What would make it faster?
    28-36Step 4, credit card. Run $1,000 at 24% on the minimum, then at a fixed $100. Try a balance where the minimum doesn't cover the interest. Who benefits from a low minimum?
    36-41Step 5, compound growth. Compare 16 and 25. Ask why the earlier saver ends with more while putting in only a little more.
    41-45Exit ticket. Students fill in "What I learned" and print it or copy the three prompts onto paper.
